Adaptive Cancer Therapy
1 / 1

1 · The problem

The drug works — then it stops working as well.

The hormone drug can shrink prostate cancer. But if the drug never stops, some cancer cells learn to live with it. Those hard-to-kill cells then grow. Then cancer becomes non-responsive to treatment. A break from the drug can help.

Adaptive Cancer Therapy

In adaptive therapy, the drug is stopped and started, instead of given all the time. This stops drug-resistant cancer cells from taking over, by not wiping out the drug-sensitive cells completely. It means less drug, a better quality of life, and cancer that takes longer to become drug-resistant.

For research only — not medical advice.

2 · Why a break can work

Two kinds of cells, one blood test.

The cancer has two kinds of cells. Green cells die when the drug is on. Red cells do not die. The two kinds fight for space to grow. The blood test only shows green plus red, added up. It cannot show the red cells alone. So we try 200 versions of the model for each patient, to show how unsure we are — most of all about how many red cells there were at the start.

Here is why a break can help: if the drug kills all green cells, red cells get all the space. On a break, green cells grow back and crowd the red ones out.

Why it matters: we have to plan drug breaks without being able to see the red cells. The model helps us guess them from the blood-test line and from when the drug was on or off.

3 · Can we trust our guesses?

How the agent makes the guess.

The agent's real job: guess whether this patient would benefit from adaptive therapy. To do that, it first guesses the hidden green/red split, again and again, until its guesses match the early tests.

The key point: the agent never sees the red cells — so it keeps 200 good guesses, not one. From them it makes its real guess: would this patient benefit from adaptive therapy, and by how much? That answer comes later, as big, middle, or small saver. The agent is Meta Muse (Muse Spark proposes each try with a Meta API key; this deck's run used a local proposer on all 105 patients, so every number can be re-made without a key).

5 · Who saves the most?

Half the drug, same control — but for who?

Loading the results…

What we found this time: the agent now ran on every patient — all 105 (Bruchovsky 72, Shaw 16, Zhang 17), not just one study. On average a break plan spends 56 of every 100 weeks off the drug, and the patients we pick spend 71 of 100 weeks off, still with the same control as never stopping (+0.3 weeks). Big savers (70+ weeks off) are 45% of our picks, against 22% of all patients. Saving still varies a lot by patient — from 6 to 99 weeks off in 100.

6 · What this would change in real life

Not longer life. An easier life.

For patients: far less time on a hard drug — fewer hot flashes, less tiredness, stronger bones — while the cancer stays under control.

For doctors: a test-based reason to say “you should take a break” to one patient, and “you should not” to another, instead of guessing.

For researchers: test the big savers first, so the next real study is smaller, faster, and more likely to give a clear answer.

Honest limit: today this changes nothing. It is a computer model, not proof in real patients. No one should change treatment because of it. A real study must prove it first.
